# ANIARA — Ship Specification

*Working reference document for screenplay development.*

---

## 1. Basic Parameters

| Property | Value |
|---|---|
| Length | ~6 km (range across concepts: 5–9 km) |
| Diameter / beam | ~1.5–2.5 km |
| Mass | on the order of 10^11 kg |
| Hull material | grown composite / living hull, not fabricated in the traditional sense — self-repairing, self-adapting |
| Population | 6,000–8,000 core crew; up to tens of thousands including passengers and generations born aboard |
| Generations aboard | 100+, drifting; no fixed terminus |
| Propulsion | distributed, field-based or integrated into the hull — no visible exhaust; movement should read as glide, not thrust |
| Destination | uncertain or symbolic (a named star system, tens of millions of light-years distant, functionally unreachable) |
| Course | uncertain |
| Purpose | to endure |

Keep the numbers loose rather than canonical. For a screenplay, precision matters far less than consistency: pick one register (a specific figure, spoken once, by one character) and don't contradict it later. Everything else can stay atmospheric.

---

## 2. Exterior Form

The hull reads as a cetacean or elasmobranch silhouette — whale, shark, or ray — arrived at by function rather than styling. It should never look like it was designed to *resemble* an animal; it should look like an engineer solved the same problem evolution solved (contain an interior, survive a hostile medium, move efficiently through it) and converged on similar answers.

- Broad rounded head or bow
- Long tapering body, smooth exterior
- A dorsal fin or stabilizer — doubles as a sensor mast / observation spine
- Ventral filaments or cilia trailing from the underside — sensory, not propulsive; read as antennae or trailing nerve-fibers
- No windows visible from outside except at a few key points (the dorsal atrium, the arboretum dome)
- Skin/hull texture: layered, scaled, or membranous, sometimes described as scarred, patched, healed — visible history of damage and repair

**Scene use:** exterior shots are your establishing loneliness beats. The ship should look small against the starfield and enormous next to a human figure in the same frame. Never both at once — choose which effect a shot needs.

## 3. Internal Structure — The Six/Seven Strata

Nearly every reference converges on a stacked, dorsal-to-ventral organization. This gives you a natural "descent" structure for blocking scenes — going down into the ship is going toward the engine of its survival; going up is going toward memory, command, and the sky.

1. **Command / Memory Nucleus** (dorsal-most)
   Navigation, sensing, long-term archives, ship's "mind." Often rendered as a small protected chamber near the spine — a place people are *summoned* to, not where they live.

2. **Culture / Habitation Decks**
   Dwellings, schools, communal rooms, libraries, gathering spaces. This is where ordinary life happens — the deck your protagonists probably live on.

3. **Life Support — Hydroponic Forests / Arboretum**
   The green band. Trees, cultivated plants, artificial ecology. Doubles as atmosphere regulation, food source, and — narratively — the one place aboard that looks like Earth. Strong contrast location: characters go here to remember, grieve, or believe in a future.

4. **Water System**
   Circulation channels, reservoirs, purification. Often visualized as vein-like branching — blue light, running water sound design. Good location for something intimate (confession, secret meeting) precisely because it's usually unstaffed.

5. **Fabrication / Repair / Material Cycling**
   Workshops, recyclers, manufacturing. Industrial, loud, functional. This is where damage gets fixed and where obsolete material gets reprocessed — thematically rich if a character or idea is being "recycled" too.

6. **Waste Recovery / Decomposition**
   Closed-loop processing. Nothing leaves, nothing is wasted — this is stated almost as scripture in several versions ("Nothing leaves. Nothing is waste. All is circulation."). A good place for a scene about what the ship *cannot* forgive or eliminate — only convert.

7. **Energy Core — "The Heart"**
   Centrally or ventrally located. A contained glow (amber, violet, or crimson depending on version), fusion or magnetic-pulse in nature. Distribution lines run outward like an arterial grid. This is the ship's single most mythologized location — treat it the way you'd treat an altar or a reactor core in a submarine film: rarely visited, heavily guarded, spoken of in half-truths by people who've never seen it.

A **central spine** runs the length of the vessel, structural, circulatory, and informational at once — keel, vertebral column, and data trunk in one. Corridors, lifts, and the main thoroughfare likely run along this spine. This gives you a single, simple mental map for blocking: everything is either *near the spine* or *out in the strata*.

## 4. The Organ Analogies (functional, not literal)

Useful as dialogue texture — the way people aboard actually talk about their own ship — but should be presented in-world as functional metaphor, not biological fact.

| System | Analog | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Hull | Skin / membrane | Boundary, protection, sensing |
| Atmosphere | Lungs | Gas exchange, climate, circulation |
| Water | Blood | Storage, purification, circulation |
| Energy core | Heart | Generation and distribution |
| Command | Nervous system | Navigation, sensing, decision |
| Hydroponics | Metabolism | Food, oxygen, ecological renewal |
| Fabrication | Muscles / skeleton | Structure, manufacture, repair |
| Recycling | Kidneys / gut | Breakdown, recovery, reuse |
| Memory / archive | Continuity | Language, culture, identity |

A recurring in-world line across versions: *we are cells in her body; her survival is ours; her journey is our home.* This is a useful register for how older generations aboard might speak, and a useful thing for a skeptical younger character to push back against.

---

## 5. The Closed Cycle

The ship's whole survival logic compresses into one loop, stated almost as doctrine:

**ENERGY → WATER → LIFE → WASTE → RECOVERY → (LIFE again)**

And five stated principles, useful as something painted on a wall, taught to children, or invoked ironically by a character who's stopped believing them:

- **Boundary** — maintain an inside.
- **Cycle** — recover what is used.
- **Differentiation** — specialized parts perform different functions.
- **Interdependence** — no subsystem survives alone.
- **Continuity** — preserve life and memory across generations.

One version renders this loop as *"A closed loop. A promise. A lie."* — which is the most dramatically useful phrasing in the whole set. The closed cycle is presented publicly as perfect and privately as leaky: something is always lost, jettisoned, or unaccounted for, and someone aboard knows it.

## 6. Command, Memory, and the Mima Question

Given your existing theoretical work on the diagnostic-boundary reading of Martinson's Mima, a screenplay set here has an obvious structural fault line already available to you: **the difference between what Command believes it can navigate by and what it can actually admit as evidence.**

Concretely, this gives you a ready-made source of dramatic tension without inventing new lore:

- A Command Nucleus that people trust as an oracle, and a memory archive underneath it that has quietly stopped producing new distinctions — repeating rather than diagnosing.
- A character (technician, archivist, low-ranking officer) who notices the difference between *the ship still functioning* and *the ship still learning* — and has to decide whether to report it.
- The "closed loop" doctrine (section 5) functioning the way institutional consensus functions in your other work — accommodation dressed as prediction, coherence mistaken for confirmation.

You don't need to import any of your formal apparatus into the dialogue. It should surface only as what characters notice and can't quite name — a technician who says "it isn't wrong, it's just not finding anything new" is doing all the philosophical work a scene needs.

## 7. Scale and Orientation for Blocking

- Human vs. city block vs. ship comparisons appear repeatedly (human ~1.8 m, city block ~300 m, Aniara ~1–9 km). Use this to remind the audience visually, early, how small a person is inside this thing — a single wide shot down one of the strata corridors does more than a spec crawl.
- "Nested scale" as an organizing visual idea: person → habitat → organ → organism. You can use this as a literal camera move — start on a face, pull back through a room, through a deck, through the strata, to the whale-shape adrift in black. One unbroken pull-back is a strong cold open or closer.

---

## 8. Tone Notes for the Screenplay

- The dominant emotional register across every version of this material is **quiet, not heroic** — an enormous, patient thing crossing near-empty space. Resist any instinct toward spectacle; the drama is interior and procedural, not explosive.
- Recurring epigraph register, useful for framing text or a character's private log: *"We voyage without shore, and drift through the night together." / "A ship of sorrows, sail on — through endless space, where no shore waits, no sun returns."* (Paraphrase any such lines rather than quoting Martinson directly if you're drawing close to the source text.)
- The ship is repeatedly described as *grown, not built* — a body that sustains its children, who in turn sustain it. This suggests dialogue where people speak of maintenance as caregiving and of failure as illness, not malfunction.
